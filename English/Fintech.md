## Chapter 2 - Agile, Scrum & Project Fundamentals

### Q7: What is Agile?

> Agile is a project management methodology that focuses on delivering software incrementally through short development cycles called sprints.
>
> It encourages collaboration, customer feedback, and continuous improvement instead of trying to deliver everything at once.

---

### Q8: What is Scrum?

> Scrum is one of the most popular Agile frameworks.
>
> It includes roles such as Product Owner, Scrum Master, and Development Team.
>
> Work is organized into sprints, typically lasting two weeks, with ceremonies such as Sprint Planning, Daily Stand-up, Sprint Review, and Sprint Retrospective.

---

### Q9: Difference between Product Owner and Project Manager?

> The Product Owner focuses on maximizing product value by defining and prioritizing the product backlog.
>
> The Project Manager focuses on project delivery, including timeline, risks, communication, resources, and stakeholder management.
>
> Although the responsibilities may overlap, their primary objectives are different.

## Chapter 4 - Microservices Architecture

### Q14: What are the benefits of using microservices?

> - **Scalability**: We can scale individual services independently based on demand, which is very efficient.
> - **Resilience**: If one microservice fails, it doesn't necessarily bring down the entire application. The other services can continue to function.
> - **Technology flexibility**: Different microservices can be developed using different programming languages or technologies, allowing teams to choose the best tool for each specific job.
> - **Faster development cycles**: Smaller, independent teams can work on different microservices simultaneously, leading to quicker releases.

## Chapter 5 - Project Management & Release

### Q15: How do you manage software releases?

> I ensure all development tasks are completed and reviewed before QA testing.
>
> After QA passes, we conduct UAT with stakeholders when required.
>
> Before deployment, I verify rollback plans, deployment procedures, and communication plans.
>
> During production deployment, I closely monitor the release and coordinate with the engineering team to address any unexpected issues quickly.

---

### Q16: How do you use Jira throughout a sprint?

> In our team, Jira is the primary tool we use to manage the entire sprint.
>
> During sprint planning, the Product Manager and Engineering Lead first define the sprint scope based on the release roadmap and business priorities. We then break down each feature into smaller development tasks and estimate the effort required for each task. The estimates are discussed with the engineering team to make sure they are realistic before assigning the tasks to developers.
>
> Throughout the sprint, every developer updates the status of their Jira tickets, such as To Do, In Progress, Code Review, QA, and Done. During our daily stand-up meetings, we review the Jira board together to understand the current progress, identify blockers, and adjust priorities if necessary.
>
> As a Technical Project Manager, I regularly monitor the Jira dashboard to track the sprint's overall health. I pay particular attention to blocked issues, overdue tasks, and the sprint burndown chart to make sure we're progressing according to plan. I also review the sprint report and team velocity to understand whether we're likely to complete the committed work.
>
> If I notice that the sprint is falling behind schedule, my first step is to identify the root cause. For example, it could be an underestimated task, a dependency on another team, or an unexpected production issue.
>
> After understanding the cause, I discuss possible solutions with the engineering lead and the Product Manager. Depending on the situation, we may reassign resources, reduce the sprint scope, split a large task into smaller ones, or negotiate moving lower-priority items to the next sprint.
>
> Throughout the process, I make sure all stakeholders receive regular updates so everyone understands the current progress, the risks, and any changes to the delivery plan.
>
> My goal is not only to ensure that the sprint finishes on time, but also to maintain transparency, remove blockers quickly, and help the team deliver high-quality software.

---

### Q17: Suppose your client wants a feature in one week, but the development team estimates three weeks.

> First, I would understand why the engineering team needs three weeks.
>
> Then I would discuss with the client to understand which requirements are truly critical.
>
> If necessary, I would negotiate a phased delivery where essential functionality is delivered first, followed by additional features later.
>
> My goal is to balance customer expectations with realistic engineering capacity while maintaining product quality.

---

### Q18: How do you handle scope creep?

> Any change request should first be evaluated for its impact on timeline, resources, budget, and technical risks.
>
> After assessing the impact, I would communicate the options to stakeholders and obtain approval before changing the project scope.
>
> This helps prevent uncontrolled project expansion.

## Chapter 6 - Conflict Resolution & Stakeholder Management

### Q19: There is a disagreement between the development team and the QA team regarding the definition of "done" for a user story.

> My first step would be to facilitate a meeting with both the development and QA leads, or even the entire teams if appropriate, to openly discuss their perspectives on the "definition of done."
>
> I would encourage them to share specific examples of where the current understanding has caused issues.
>
> Then, my goal would be to guide them towards a mutually agreed-upon definition that clearly outlines all the necessary steps for a task to be considered complete.
>
> This agreed definition would then be documented and consistently applied to all future sprints.

---

### Q20: Tell me about a time when there was a disagreement between the engineering team and the business team.

> Yes, I experienced a similar situation in one of our product releases.
>
> We had a feature that was planned for an upcoming release because it was important for the business timeline. However, during the final testing phase, the engineering team identified several issues that could affect system stability under certain conditions. Although the feature worked in most scenarios, we weren't confident enough to release it to production.
>
> My first step was to understand the technical risks. I met with the engineering team to determine the severity of the issues, whether there were any workarounds, and how much time would be needed to fix them.
>
> Then, I discussed the situation with the Product Manager and other stakeholders. Instead of simply saying, "We can't release," I explained the potential business impact of releasing an unstable feature, including possible production incidents, customer complaints, and emergency rollbacks.
>
> At the same time, I tried to find alternatives. For example, we discussed whether we could reduce the scope, delay only that feature while releasing everything else, or use a feature flag so the code could be deployed without being exposed to users.
>
> After reviewing the risks and the available options, the stakeholders agreed that delaying that feature was the best decision. We updated the release plan, communicated the new timeline to everyone involved, and focused on fixing the remaining issues.
>
> In the end, the feature was released in the following sprint without any production incidents, and the stakeholders appreciated the transparency throughout the decision-making process.
>
> One lesson I learned is that a Technical Project Manager should never simply choose the engineering side or the business side. The role is to facilitate communication, make the risks clear, and help everyone make an informed decision based on facts rather than assumptions.

>
> If needed, we would organize a technical review involving other senior engineers.
>
> The final decision should be based on technical merit and business value rather than personal preference.

## Chapter 7 - Risk Management & Production Incidents

### Q23: Imagine production is down. What will you do?

> First, I would assess the severity of the incident and gather initial information.
>
> Next, I would notify the relevant stakeholders and assemble the appropriate technical team.
>
> I would assign clear responsibilities, monitor the investigation, and provide regular status updates to clients and management.
>
> After the issue is resolved, I would organize a post-incident review to identify the root cause and implement preventive measures.

---

### Q24: Imagine a critical bug is discovered in production just hours before a major product launch.

> First, I would immediately inform stakeholders about the critical bug and its potential impact on the launch.
>
> We would then collaboratively decide whether to proceed with the release or postpone it, depending on the severity of the bug.
>
> Simultaneously, I would assemble the technical team to diagnose and resolve the issue with the highest priority.
>
> If the bug can be fixed promptly, we would aim to proceed with the planned release timeline.

---

### Q25: Imagine you're managing two high-priority projects at the same time. How would you prioritize?

> First, I would assess the impact of both issues. For the production incident, I would determine how many customers are affected, whether payments are failing, and whether there are any security or compliance risks. At the same time, I would understand the business importance of the new feature and whether the delivery deadline is flexible.
>
> Next, I would organize a discussion with the Product Manager, the engineering lead, and other key stakeholders to review the situation. I believe everyone should have the same understanding of the risks and the available options.
>
> Since the production issue is already affecting existing customers, I would normally prioritize resolving it first. If possible, I would assign the engineers most familiar with that system to handle the incident while allowing the remaining team members to continue working on the new feature.
>
> If the team doesn't have enough capacity, I would discuss adjusting the scope or timeline of the new feature with the Product Manager. Rather than simply saying, "We have to delay," I would explain the reasons, the expected impact, and propose a revised delivery plan.
>
> Throughout the process, I would keep stakeholders updated with regular progress reports so everyone understands the current status and the next steps.
>
> My goal is to minimize customer impact while maintaining transparent communication and making decisions based on both technical and business priorities.

---

### Q26: How do you identify, assess, and manage project risks?

> I believe that risk management is one of the most important responsibilities of a Technical Project Manager. My approach is to identify risks as early as possible, continuously monitor them, and work with the team to reduce their impact before they become actual issues.
>
> I usually classify risks into three categories: technical risks, project risks, and business risks.
>
> **Technical risks** include things like complex third-party integrations, performance issues, or unexpected technical challenges during development.
>
> **Project risks** include schedule delays, resource constraints, unclear requirements, or dependencies on other teams.
>
> **Business risks** include compliance requirements, customer commitments, or changes in business priorities that could affect the release plan.
>
> To identify these risks early, I don't wait until the end of the sprint. I monitor them throughout the project by participating in sprint planning, daily stand-up meetings, backlog refinement sessions, and regular discussions with the engineering team and Product Manager.
>
> Once a risk is identified, I evaluate both its likelihood and its potential impact on the project. Based on that assessment, I work with the team to define a mitigation plan. For example, we may break a complex feature into smaller deliverables, assign additional engineering support, schedule earlier testing, or reduce the scope of the release if necessary.
>
> Communication is also very important. I make sure stakeholders understand the risks, the mitigation plan, and any impact on the project timeline. I prefer to communicate risks early rather than surprise stakeholders at the last minute.
>
> One example from my previous project involved a feature that depended on a third-party service. During development, we realized the integration API wasn't as stable as expected, which created a risk to our release schedule.
>
> I organized a discussion with the engineering team and the Product Manager to assess the impact. Together, we decided to separate the feature into two phases so that the core functionality could still be released on time while the integration improvements were completed in the following sprint.
>
> As a result, we met the release deadline without compromising system stability, and we completed the remaining work in the next release.
>
> From that experience, I learned that successful risk management is not about eliminating every risk. It's about identifying risks early, communicating transparently, and making informed decisions before they become production problems.

## Closing Thoughts

> **Key Principles for a Technical Project Manager in Fintech:**
>
> 1. **Balance technical depth with business acumen** — understand both the code and the market.
> 2. **Communicate transparently** — never hide risks or delays from stakeholders.
> 3. **Prioritize stability over speed** — in financial systems, a production incident can be catastrophic.
> 4. **Empower the team** — delegation and mentoring scale better than micromanagement.
> 5. **Document everything** — technical design, decisions, and "definition of done" must be clear to all.
